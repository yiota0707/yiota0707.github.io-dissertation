Research background

Why cooperation is difficult for learning agents

Individually rational behaviour does not always produce the best outcome for a group. In social dilemmas, agents can receive an immediate advantage by acting selfishly even though sustained cooperation would create a better collective outcome.

Research problem

Can interaction structure help cooperation emerge?

Standard reinforcement-learning agents optimise behaviour using experienced rewards. In a Prisoner's Dilemma, defection provides a strong short-term incentive, making persistent cooperation difficult to learn.

This dissertation studies whether agents can overcome this difficulty when they can change interaction partners and when rematching favours agents with similar recent behaviour.

C

Cooperation

A cooperative action can produce a strong mutual benefit, but it also exposes an agent to exploitation by a defector.

D

Defection

Defection can maximise an immediate individual payoff, particularly when the interaction partner cooperates.

m

Assortative rematching

Assortativity changes who interacts with whom by increasing the probability that agents with similar previous actions are paired.

S

In the hybrid model, agents learn whether to retain their current partner or enter the rematching pool.

Model architecture

A controlled baseline and an adaptive hybrid model

Both models use the same repeated Prisoner's Dilemma learning environment. Their central difference is whether agents learn a partner-retention and switching policy.

Controlled comparison

Baseline model

Baseline

The baseline isolates the effect of assortative rematching. Agents learn their Prisoner's Dilemma actions but do not learn a separate stay-or-switch policy.

  1. Agents repeatedly interact with a current partner.
  2. Prisoner's Dilemma actions are selected using learned action values.
  3. After an interaction block, agents enter the rematching process.
  4. Rematching is random with probability \(1-m\) and assortative with probability \(m\).
Experimental purpose

Tests whether assortative rematching alone is sufficient to sustain cooperation.

Proposed combined mechanism

Hybrid model

Hybrid

The hybrid combines learned partner retention and switching with random or assortative rematching.

  1. Agents repeatedly interact with their current partner.
  2. Agents learn Prisoner's Dilemma action values.
  3. Agents also learn whether to stay with or switch away from their current partner.
  4. Agents entering the rematching pool are paired randomly with probability \(1-m\) or assortatively with probability \(m\).
Experimental purpose

Tests whether adaptive partner mobility and behavioural similarity reinforce one another.

When switch-policy learning was disabled, stay and switch values remained tied and agents switched approximately half of the time. The result indicates that frequent movement is not enough: agents must learn when to retain a productive partner and when to leave an unproductive one.

Learning-speed analysis

Faster convergence did not mean better cooperation

At \(m=1\), the baseline stabilised much earlier than the hybrid, but it stabilised at a substantially lower level of cooperation.

The hybrid required more training to develop its adaptive interaction structure. Its slower convergence was associated with a much stronger long-term cooperative outcome.

Overall interpretation

Assortativity alone does not explain the high cooperation

1

The baseline provides a controlled test of assortative rematching without learned partner switching.

2

The full hybrid combines future-oriented action learning with adaptive partner retention and switching.

3

Removing either future-return learning or learned partner switching substantially changes the cooperative outcome.

4

Cooperative policies such as Always Cooperate and Tit-for-Tat were more prominent in the full hybrid, while ablated conditions became dominated by defective or tied policies.

Scope and limitations

Interpreting the findings carefully

The results provide evidence about cooperation within the implemented computational environment. They should not be interpreted as direct evidence about human behaviour or every possible multi-agent learning system.

01

Simplified environment

The study uses a repeated two-action Prisoner's Dilemma and therefore abstracts from many complexities of real-world interaction.

02

Specific learning architecture

Findings apply to the implemented Q-learning agents, state representation, payoff structure and interaction mechanisms.

03

Simulation evidence

Experimental conclusions are based on simulated populations rather than physical or human multi-agent systems.

04

Parameter-dependent behaviour

Cooperation can change across learning rates, exploration temperatures, population sizes and training horizons.

05

Browser visualisation

The website simulation simplifies the research implementation to make its central mechanisms accessible and interactive.

06

No universal optimum claimed

Sensitivity analyses test robustness. They do not establish a universally optimal hyperparameter configuration.
